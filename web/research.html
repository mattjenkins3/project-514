Training research

How we translate climbing experiments, endurance studies and athlete logs into a weekly prescription.

The ten-minute no-hang routine

10 minutes per session · twice daily on training days. Keep both feet on the floor and pull gently on a comfortable 18–22 mm edge.

  • Every repetition — pull lightly for 10 seconds, then rest for 20 seconds.
  • 6 repetitions — regular four-finger grip.
  • 6 repetitions — open-hand grip with the front three fingers.
  • 2 repetitions — front two fingers, open hand.
  • 2 repetitions — middle two fingers, open hand.
  • 2 repetitions — front two fingers, half crimp.
  • 2 repetitions — middle two fingers, half crimp.

Total — 20 repetitions, 10 minutes. Repeat at least six hours later. Also leave six hours from climbing or heavy finger work. Keep tension light and skip painful or sore fingers. Rest days have no finger sessions.

What the evidence supports

  • Emil’s twice-daily routine is an athlete protocol. The app uses this cadence on active days for the no-hangs-only option.
  • Gilmore and colleagues compared self-selected training logs over 4–16 weeks. Light loading and max hangs were associated with strength gains, with the largest gains in the combined group.
  • The retrospective study did not establish an optimal frequency or measure injury prevention. Adjust tension and skip painful sessions.

Heavy fingers, fresh fingers

  • Two weekly hangboard sessions improved climbing-specific strength in a ten-week controlled study of intermediate and advanced climbers.
  • A separate comparison in advanced climbers tested maximal and intermittent hangs with distinct rest intervals.
  • These studies concern specific strength and endurance tests.
  • Transfer to a route grade depends on movement, tactics and practice.
  • Our conservative starting point is one heavy session weekly in a loading week, with full rests.
  • Combined programs place light sessions on other climbing days.
  • Recovery weeks omit heavy finger work.
  • The no-hangs-only option retains light sessions on active days. Ten-week randomized trial · Comparison of hang protocols

Build volume, then absorb it

  • Seiler and Kjerland observed predominantly easy endurance training in trained skiers.
  • Neal and colleagues found benefits from a polarized distribution in a small crossover trial of trained cyclists.
  • Esteve-Lanao and colleagues also compared below-threshold and threshold-heavy distributions in distance runners. Read the running trial.
  • Most cardio here stays conversational so athletes can combine it with climbing.
  • No single percentage or four-week recovery schedule was validated across these sports.
  • Mountain runner and ski mountaineer Kilian Jornet describes varying terrain, specificity and volume across his summer season.
  • We translate that into sustained uphill walking for mountain goals, easy continuous riding for cyclists and run/walk sessions for runners.
  • In his team’s training notes, Alberto Bettiol describes alternating two or three training days with an easy ride or a day off.
  • This informs our decision to retain recovery even when a target date is close.
  • Our sessions offer familiar food examples and opportunities to practice on longer outings. Jornet’s training diary · Seiler and Kjerland · Neal et al. · Bettiol on recovery

Strength that evolves with your week

Every non-climbing day retains strength work. Two sessions in a rolling seven-day window can be demanding, separated by at least two calendar days. Other days use short, easy support circuits.

Weeks What changes
1–3 Learn the A/B movements. Start with one set, then use two. Build controlled repetitions.
4 & 8 One easy set of support movements. Keep plenty of repetitions in reserve.
5–7 Build toward three sets where recovery allows. Harder aerobic days get light strength.
9–11 Return to two sets, with a lower repetition range on main exercises.
12 Reduce fatigue with easy support work before reassessing.
  • Main A/B sessions repeat pushing, rowing, trunk and leg patterns. Easy circuits rotate shoulders, hips and balance.
  • Whole-body training adds more leg and hip work. Upper-body/core keeps a smaller lower-body dose.
  • Finish main sets with 2–3 repetitions available. Support days leave 4–5 or more. Soreness, difficult recent sessions and next-day climbing reduce the demand.
  • First improve repetitions and technique, then choose a slightly more challenging resistance. The app leaves weight selection to you.
  • The exact schedule and exercise mix are coaching interpretations of the evidence. Their combined effect has not been tested as one program.

What the calendar estimates

  • Weeks 1–3 establish capacity, week 4 unloads, weeks 5–7 build toward the longest endurance week, and week 8 unloads again.
  • The final weeks shift toward project practice and assessment.
  • These multipliers are coaching choices.
  • Reports of fatigue reduce upcoming loads.
  • A target date expresses intent.
  • After comparable improving benchmarks at least two weeks apart, the outlook extrapolates your observed pace.
  • Grades are ordinal, and progress can stall or accelerate.

Strength, progression & fatigue

  1. ACSM 2026 position stand — resistance training prescription

    Overview of reviews. Grounds the progression, effort and set-count principles.

  2. ACSM 2009 — progression models for healthy adults

    Position stand. A foundation for staged progression and rest intervals.

  3. Currier et al. — resistance training prescriptions

    Systematic review and network meta-analysis comparing load, sets and frequency.

  4. Moesgaard et al. — periodization with volume equated

    Meta-analysis. Supports structured changes in volume and effort; the exact app cycle is a coaching choice.

  5. Kassiano et al. — systematic exercise variation

    Systematic review. Informs repeated A/B movements and limited variation instead of constant exercise replacement.

  6. Grgic et al. — failure versus non-failure training

    Meta-analysis. Training to failure is not required for strength development.

  7. Robinson et al. — proximity to failure and adaptations

    Exploratory meta-regressions. Informs effort guidance while acknowledging uncertainty around the ideal reserve.

  8. Halperin et al. — controlling and reporting proximity to failure

    Review. Repetitions in reserve are useful but subjective.

  9. Grgic et al. — resistance training frequency and strength

    Meta-analysis. Frequency must be interpreted alongside total training volume.

  10. Ralston et al. — weekly training frequency effects

    Meta-analysis. More sessions alone do not establish a superior strength stimulus.

  11. Cuthbert et al. — frequency in well-trained populations

    Systematic review and meta-analysis of volume-matched frequency comparisons.

  12. Schoenfeld et al. — volume, hypertrophy and strength

    Controlled trial. Higher volume did not improve every outcome, supporting restraint with added lifting.

  13. Bell et al. — international deloading consensus

    Delphi consensus. Supports planned fatigue management; expert agreement is distinct from trial evidence.

  14. Deloading practices in strength and physique sports

    Cross-sectional survey. Describes real-world practice and the limits of current evidence.

  15. Gaining more from doing less? A one-week deload trial

    Controlled study. Complete cessation was not universally beneficial; our recovery weeks retain easy movement.

Climbing & finger training

  1. Langer, Simon & Wiemeyer — strength training in climbing

    Systematic review. Climbing outcomes depend on the training task and available intervention evidence.

  2. Effects of climbing and resistance training on climbing-specific performance

    Systematic review and meta-analysis. Supports combining movement practice and targeted strength.

  3. The connection between resistance training and climbing performance

    Narrative review. Useful context for climbing-specific strength and the limits of injury-prevention claims.

  4. Gilmore, Klimek, Abrahamsson & Baar — different finger-loading programs

    2024 retrospective study. Basis for the light no-hang routine; it does not prove an optimal daily frequency.

  5. Ten weeks of hangboard training

    Controlled intervention. Supports finger-strength work with attention to the studied training dose.

  6. Hangboard training in advanced climbers

    Randomized controlled trial. Examines progressive added-load training over eight weeks.

  7. Comparison of maximal and intermittent hang protocols

    Protocol comparison. Informs the distinction between strength and endurance finger sessions.

  8. High resistance–few repetitions versus low resistance–high repetitions

    Climbing intervention. Performance transfer and training-test improvements can differ.

Endurance & concurrent training

  1. Schumann et al. — compatibility of aerobic and strength training

    Updated meta-analysis. Concurrent training can work; sequencing and fatigue still warrant care.

  2. Llanos-Lagos et al. — strength training and running economy

    Systematic review and meta-analysis. Effects vary with strength method and running speed.

  3. Llanos-Lagos et al. — heavy strength training in cyclists

    Meta-analysis. Suggests performance benefits with low certainty and no consistent VO₂max improvement.

  4. Comparison of short-sprint and heavy strength training in cyclists

    Controlled comparison. Illustrates that adaptations depend on the specific training stimulus.

  5. Seiler & Kjerland — intensity distribution in endurance athletes

    Observational endurance research. Informs the large proportion of easy aerobic work.

  6. Neal et al. — polarized versus threshold training

    Cycling intervention. Relevant to intensity balance rather than a universal training ratio.

  7. Esteve-Lanao et al. — training intensity distribution in runners

    Running intervention. Informs the emphasis on sustainable easy work.
